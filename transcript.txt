Have you ever noticed something strange about yourself?
Around your family, you behave one way.
Around your friends, you become a completely different person.
And when you are with someone you truly love,
there is another side of you that nobody else gets to see.
Now, tell me something.
Which one of these is the real you?
Think about it for a moment.
Are you the quiet person your family knows,
the funny person your friends laugh with,
or the sensitive person who sometimes lies awake at night,
thinking about things they never tell anyone.
Perhaps the answer is not as simple as we think.
You see, when I was younger, I used to think that every person had one
personality, one fixed nature, one way of being.
But the more you observe people, the more you begin to wonder.
Why does a person become confident around one group, but nervous around another?
Why does someone speak freely with friends, but stay silent at home?
And why can the same person feel strong in one relationship,
yet feel completely insecure in another?
Maybe we have been asking the wrong question.
Perhaps the question is not just, who am I?
Perhaps we should also ask, who do I become around the people in my life?
Let me explain.
Imagine a young boy sitting in a classroom.
He has plenty of ideas.
He wants to ask questions.
He wants to speak.
But whenever he says something, his classmates laugh at him.
So, slowly, he becomes quiet.
Years pass.
Now imagine that this same boy meets a group of people who listen to him.
They take his ideas seriously.
They encourage him to speak.
Suddenly, he begins talking more.
He shares his thoughts.
He becomes confident.
Now, tell me, was the boy always shy, or
did he learn to hide a part of himself?
Of course, we cannot explain a person's entire personality through one experience.
Human behavior is much more complicated than that.
But research in personality psychology gives us an interesting clue.
People can behave differently in different situations,
while still having stable personality traits.
In other words, you can be the same person and still act differently,
depending on where you are, whom you are with, and how you feel in that moment.
And here is where things become interesting.
Because sometimes, the people around us do not simply see who we are.
Their reactions may also influence who we slowly learn to become.
Think about a child.
A child does not arrive in this world knowing how relationships work.
They learn.
When they are sad, does somebody listen?
When they make a mistake, are they guided with patience or made to feel ashamed?
When they express an opinion, are they allowed to speak?
Little by little, these experiences can shape what a child expects from
other people.
Psychologists John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth help develop attachment
theory, which explores how early relationships with caregivers
relate to a child's sense of security and their ways of seeking comfort.
And research has found that attachment patterns show some stability over time.
But listen carefully to this part.
Stability does not mean permanence.
A difficult childhood does not decide your entire future.
A supportive childhood does not guarantee that every later relationship
will be healthy.
People grow.
Circumstances change.
We meet new people, experience new things, and sometimes learn a
completely different way of relating to others.
Your childhood may be part of your story.
It does not have to be the final chapter.
And as we grow older, something else begins to shape us, our friends.
Now, I want you to think about your closest group of friends.
When you are with them, what feels normal?
What do you talk about?
What do you laugh at?
What kinds of behavior do you accept without even thinking about it?
Imagine one group where everyone makes fun of people who ask questions.
After some time, you might hesitate before asking one yourself.
Now imagine another group where people enjoy learning, sharing ideas,
and admitting when they do not know something.
In that group, curiosity may feel natural.
Do you see the difference?
The people around us can influence what feels normal.
Research on friendships shows that two things can happen.
First, we often choose friends who are already similar to us.
Second, friends can influence each other over time.
So, sometimes we become more like our friends.
And sometimes we were already alike, which is why we became friends
in the first place.
The truth is, it can be a little of both.
But please, do not misunderstand this idea.
A good friend does not always have to make you more productive,
more successful, or more ambitious.
Some friends help you think better.
Some help you laugh when life becomes difficult.
And some simply sit beside you when you have nothing to say.
That matters too.
A relationship is not valuable only because of what it helps you achieve.
Sometimes it is valuable because you can finally stop pretending.
And that brings us to something even more personal, family.
You see, family is often where we first learn how people deal with anger,
affection, disagreement, and forgiveness.
Imagine growing up in a home where people can disagree
without insulting each other.
You may learn that disagreement is uncomfortable,
but it does not have to destroy a relationship.
Now imagine growing up where every disagreement becomes a fight,
or where silence is used to punish someone.
A person in that environment may learn to avoid conflict,
even when something important needs to be said.
Of course, families are different,
and a person's behavior can have many causes.
But familiar patterns can follow us into adulthood.
Sometimes we repeat what we learned.
Sometimes we spend years trying not to repeat it.
And sometimes we do not even realize that the way we respond today
has something to do with the way we learned to respond yesterday.
Think about that for a moment.
Perhaps the way you handle criticism was learned somewhere.
Perhaps the way you ask for help was learned somewhere.
Perhaps even the way you express love
was influenced by what you saw growing up.
But remember what we discovered earlier?
Our past influences us.
It does not completely control us.
And this becomes especially important when we fall in love.
Imagine telling your partner
about something that makes you feel afraid.
You are not asking them to fix your life.
You simply want them to understand.
If they listen with care,
you may slowly become more comfortable opening up.
But if your feelings are repeatedly dismissed
or used against you, you may begin to hide them.
Relationship researchers study something called
partner responsiveness.
It is a simple idea.
Do you feel understood by your partner?
Do you feel that your feelings matter?
Do you feel cared for even when the two of you disagree?
A healthy relationship does not mean
two people agree on everything.
It means they can disagree without forgetting
that they are speaking to a human being they care about.
And over time, this kind of environment
can influence how comfortable people feel
being honest, vulnerable, and open with one another.
But there is something we must be careful about.
Your partner is not responsible
for fixing every wound you carry from the past.
And you are not responsible
for becoming someone else's entire world.
Love can provide support.
It cannot replace personal responsibility,
mutual respect, or the work that each person
must do for themselves.
So, where does all this leave us?
Our family influences us.
Our friends influence us.
Our partners influence us.
But are we simply the product of the people around us?
No.
Two people can grow up in the same house
and become very different adults.
Two friends can spend years together
and still have different values.
And two people can experience the same event
but understand it in completely different ways.
We bring our own temperament, choices,
experiences, and beliefs into every relationship.
The influence goes both ways.
We shape our relationships and our relationships shape us.
Perhaps this is the part of human nature we often overlook.
We spend so much time asking ourselves
whether we are good enough,
confident enough, or strong enough.
But we rarely stop and ask,
what happens to me around the people
I spend my life with?
Can I speak honestly around them?
Can I make a mistake without feeling worthless?
Can I disagree without being afraid?
Can I grow without being made to feel guilty for changing?
These questions are worth asking.
Not because every relationship must be perfect.
Not because we should abandon everyone who challenges us.
But because understanding yourself
also means understanding the world
in which you are trying to become yourself.
And now let us return to the question we began with.
Which version of you is the real one?
The quiet person at home?
The laughing person among friends?
The confident person at work?
Or the vulnerable person who shares their fears
with someone they love?
Maybe all of them are real.
You are not required to behave
exactly the same way in every situation.
There is a difference between adapting
to the people around you
and losing yourself to please them.
You can be gentle with one person,
playful with another, and serious with someone else.
That does not make you fake.
But if you constantly hide what you believe,
fear expressing what you feel,
or become someone you do not recognize
just to be accepted,
perhaps that is something worth understanding.
And here is one last thought
I want you to carry with you.
Do not only ask what the people in your life
are doing for you.
Ask what your relationships are making possible.
Are they giving you room to become more honest,
more thoughtful, more understanding?
And just as importantly,
what are you making possible for the people around you?
